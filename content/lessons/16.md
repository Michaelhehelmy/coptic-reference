# Lesson 16 — The circumstance before the main clause

Fifteen lessons ended with Ruth read whole. The course does not stop
there: the next five lessons take the grammar you have and hand it the
hardest Coptic in the project — Shenoute, a fourth-century Egyptian monk
whose discourses ship as the seventh corpus work. Before his sentences,
one more piece of machinery: how a Sahidic sentence puts its circumstance
in front of its main clause.

The circumstance wears one of two words. The first is ⲉⲣⲉ — English
"when, while, if". It opens a subordinate clause, the main clause answers
it, and the pair reads as one sentence: setting first, event second.
Shenoute's verse 309 opens with ⲉⲣⲉ and the sentence does not let go
until the verse is over — one frame holding the whole run. In the window
below you meet the same family in its conditional dress: ⲉⲣϣⲁⲛ (if)
opens verse 95's famous line, "if man gains the whole world, he loses
his soul" — condition first, consequence second, exactly the order the
converter sets. (That quotation is the corpus's own English, attached to
the Coptic beside it.)

The second circumstance word is ⲙⲡⲁⲧⲉ — English "before" — which
fixes the earlier of two events. Shenoute uses it only twice in this
work: at verse 75, inside your drill range, where a man is ashamed
before he receives, and once at verse 357. Both carry no English at
all — read them as pure Coptic. Neither ⲉⲣⲉ nor the before-word has a
dictionary headword, which is why both sit in this lesson's
`unlisted_forms`, as lesson 8's converter did — the pattern they build
is what you learn, not the tokens themselves.

The adverbs that steer the whole clause are your vocabulary. ⲙⲁⲗⲗⲟⲛ
(more, especially), ⲡⲁⲗⲓⲛ (again, once more), ⲕⲁⲗⲱⲥ (rightly, well),
ⲉⲧⲓ (still), ⲁⲗⲏⲑⲱⲥ (truly), ⲙⲙⲁⲧⲉ (only), ⲗⲟⲓⲡⲟⲛ (from now on,
henceforth), ⲣⲱ (same, again, also), ⲙⲁⲗⲓⲥⲧⲁ (especially), ⲉⲙⲁⲧⲉ
(greatly, very), ϣⲁϩⲣⲁⲓ (upward), ⲑⲱⲛ (where), ⲧⲱⲛ (where), ⲙⲟⲛⲟⲛ
(only, simply, alone). Each one lands on the sentence as a whole rather
than on the noun beside it — that is what makes it an adverb here, and
it is why a clause framed by ⲉⲣⲉ can still be steered by a single word.

Pass the intermediate adverb quiz at 70%, drill Shenoute 0–119, then
read Shenoute 95–98 — English sits beside the Coptic in every verse of
the window — and find where the circumstance stands before its main
clause. Lesson 17 hangs the noun phrase on the same frame: the
prepositional phrase, and the possessions it carries.
