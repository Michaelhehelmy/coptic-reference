# Lesson 5 — Negation and number

Coptic negation wraps the clause: ⲛ before the verb, ⲁⲛ after it. Both
pieces are real corpus tokens — search ⲁⲛ in the reader and see how often
it closes a clause it did not open. Till's grammar chapter on negation is
short because the pattern is regular; the drills will make it automatic.

Counting is where Coptic shows its Egyptian roots. ⲟⲩⲁⲓ is "one" and
ⲥⲛⲁⲩ is "two" — both carry those glosses as dictionary headwords, both
appear in Ruth's household arithmetic (two sons, one dead husband each).
ⲙⲏϣ is the crowd word ("multitude, crowd", and as an adjective "many, a
lot"): Ruth's ⲙⲏϣ moments are the ones where the village reacts.

Your ten words: ⲟⲛ (again, also, still), ⲙⲙⲁⲩ (there, therein), ⲟⲩⲁⲓ
(one), ⲙⲏϣ (multitude, many), ⲥⲛⲁⲩ (two), ⲣⲁⲛ (name), ϣⲉⲗⲉⲉⲧ (bride,
daughter-in-law), ⲥϩⲓⲙⲉ (woman, wife), ⲣⲱⲙⲉ (man), ⲥⲟⲛ (brother). Ruth is
about both brides and daughters-in-law, so read both glosses of ϣⲉⲗⲉⲉⲧ.

Pass the beginner adverb quiz at 70%, drill Mark 30–69, then read Mark
1:5–8. You now own the hundred most useful words of two dialects — lesson
6 starts pronouns, and the sentences get longer.

A note on counting: Coptic numbers agree with what they count the way adjectives do, which lesson 2 already taught you to watch. When a drill blanks a numeral, read the noun first — the noun tells you what is being counted, and the numeral only says how many.
